How to use this

The whole method fits in one screen. Read it once, then never think about it again — just work one lesson at a time, at your own pace.

🔊 Tap any Korean to hear it

Every Korean word, phrase, example, and letter — in the daily lessons and in the reference tables — is a button. Tap or click it to hear it spoken, then read along. Audio uses your device's built-in Korean voice; if nothing plays, a note appears at the top with how to add one. Works on computer, phone, and iPad.

If a word isn't clear, three options at the top bar: flip Slow audio for a slower reading, flip Repeat ×3 to hear it three times in a row, or switch voice if your device offers more than one Korean voice (the picker only shows up when it does). Every vocabulary word's "Say" column also carries a small Thai reading under the English romanization — a third anchor for the sound, alongside the audio.

Freezing on "Build it yourself"? Read this first.

If you open a lesson and lock up trying to make a sentence — stop. That is the advanced version. The real exercise is step ① of the practice box: play each 🔊 line and say it straight back. Pure imitation, no composing. That is a complete lesson. Building your own sentences comes weeks later, once dozens of models are in your ear. Never let step ③ block you — skip it and move on.

Each lesson, ~15–25 minutes:

  1. Warm up (2 min) — one Hangul trainer round, then reopen yesterday's lesson and say its lines once.
  2. New words (5 min) — tap through the table, say each word aloud three times, matching the audio.
  3. Pattern (5 min) — read the pattern and its examples, tapping every 🔊.
  4. Practice (5 min) — do step ① (repeat after the audio). Do step ② (change one word) if you have room. Do step ③ (make your own sentence) only when it already feels easy.
  5. Bank it (2 min) — add today's words to a flashcard app (Anki, Quizlet) and review the deck daily.

Pace it like this:

  • It's 60 lessons, not 60 fixed days. Repeat any lesson for a 2nd or 3rd day until it feels easy. Being "behind" the number is fine — skipping the audio is not.
  • Phase 1 especially: stay on a letter-lesson two days if the shapes aren't automatic. The trainer is your daily Hangul workout.
  • Never read Korean silently. Mouth not moving = it didn't happen.
  • Trust the Hangul over the romanization — the "say" column fades from your head by Week 3.
  • Miss a day? Half-session. Just don't skip two in a row.
  • Every 5th–7th lesson, take a pure review day: re-run the last milestone instead of new material.
  • From Phase 4, add 5 minutes of shadowing: play any Korean audio and talk along one second behind, copying the rhythm.

What "survival level" means here

By Day 60 you will read fluently, understand slow speech and fixed service phrases, and speak in short polite sentences: greetings, ordering, shopping, directions, taxis, times and prices, asking for help, and basic small talk. That is roughly the spoken range of TOPIK level 1. It is not fluency — it is not being stuck.

English or Thai for pronunciation?

Keep learning in English — it's the right call, and you get English practice for free. Romanization of any language is only a temporary crutch: once you read Hangul directly (around Week 2–3) you stop going through it at all, and the confusion goes away.

Every "Say" column shows both: English romanization on top, a Thai reading underneath. There are 7 sounds (listed under the trainer below) where the Thai line is genuinely more accurate than the English — for everything else either is a fine guide. Whichever you read, the rule stays the same: copy the 🔊 audio, not the spelling.

Verb builder 동사 만들기

Korean verbs change by adding an ending, the same way English adds “ing” or “ed”. This tool shows how, one step at a time. Pick a verb, then tap 🔊 on any result.

The idea, in English first. Take "to go". Remove the word "to" and you have go. Now you can build: going, goes, I want to go. The part that stays the same is go.

Korean works the same way. Every verb in a dictionary ends in 다, and it means "to …". Cut off 다 and the part that is left is called the stem. The stem is the part that stays the same. Then you add an ending to it.

Pronunciation rules 발음

Korean spelling stays constant; the sounds shift at block boundaries. These eight rules cover almost everything you'll hear.

  1. Linking — a final consonant slides onto a following empty ㅇ. 음악 → [으막], 한국어 → [한구거], 밥이 → [바비].
  2. Final neutralization — at the end of a block ㅅㅆㅈㅊㅌㅎ → [t], ㅋㄲ → [k], ㅍ → [p]. 옷 [ot], 꽃 [kkot].
  3. Nasalization — ㄱㄷㅂ before ㄴ/ㅁ turn into [ng/n/m]. 감사합니다 → [감사함니다], 먹는 → [멍는].
  4. ㅎ weakening — ㅎ between voiced sounds nearly vanishes. 전화 → [저놔], 은행 → [으냉].
  5. Tensification — a plain consonant after a [k/t/p] final goes tense. 학교 → [학꾜], 식당 → [식땅].
  6. ㄴ ↔ ㄹ — when ㄴ and ㄹ meet, both become [l]. 신라 → [실라], 일 년 → [일련].
  7. Palatalization — 디/티 + 이 → [지/치]. 같이 → [가치], 굳이 → [구지].
  8. ㅢ — [ui] only at the start of a word; [i] elsewhere; [e] when it means "'s". 의사 [의사], 회의 [회이], 나의 [나에].

Use Sino-Korean (일 이 삼…) for: money, dates, months, minutes, phone numbers, addresses, counting above ~20.

Use Native Korean (하나 둘 셋…) for: the hour, your age, and counting things or people with a counter. Before a counter, 하나/둘/셋/넷/스물 shorten to 한/두/세/네/스무.
